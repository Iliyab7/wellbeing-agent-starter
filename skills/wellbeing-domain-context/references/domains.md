# Domain methods for the nonmedical pilot

## Sleep and daily rhythm

Possible authorized inputs: self-reported sleep/wake times or quality, recorded duration,
and relevant schedule context. Clarify whether a field represents time in bed, estimated
sleep or another definition; do not silently treat them as the same. Handle overnight
dates/timezones and missing nights. Describe coverage and comparable personal changes.
Treat a calendar's early meeting as context, not proof of sleep loss or its cause.

Coach may help adapt the participant's chosen evening/morning routine to known practical
constraints, or help them select what they want to observe. Do not assign universal sleep
targets, diagnose a disorder or propose a medication/supplement change. If a concern
depends on clinical interpretation, explain the limit and encourage appropriate human care.

## Everyday activity and recovery context

Possible inputs: chosen activity goal, optional steps/minutes, self-report, planned events
and any participant-stated professional restrictions. Keep recorded movement, a scheduled
workout and actual reported completion separate. Check duplicate sources and changed
tracking methods before concluding activity changed. A reduction does not prove injury,
deconditioning, low motivation or a need to increase training.

Coach may help fit an already-chosen routine into the person's available time and
preferences. Do not prescribe load, intensity, rehabilitation or return-to-sport decisions.
Relevant medical restrictions are constraints to respect, not instructions for the AI to
reinterpret. Separate human coaches remain external recipients with their own sharing scope.

## Nutrition and hydration logs

Possible inputs: optional participant-selected simple logs, a chosen everyday goal,
constraints/preferences and the source's definitions. Absence of a logged meal/drink is
not evidence it was missed. Estimates and incomplete logs must retain their uncertainty.
Do not derive nutritional sufficiency, disease, weight judgments or eating-disorder labels
from sparse records. Do not request weight/calorie information merely to fill a template.

Coach may help make the participant's chosen nonclinical routine easier to remember or
carry out. Do not generate restrictive diets, calorie/weight targets, treatment diets or
fluid prescriptions. If clinical constraints or distress make generic habit help unsuitable,
limit the service and seek appropriate human support.

## Self-reported stress, energy and everyday mood

Possible inputs: optional self-report and authorized relevant context. Keep scale labels
and direction consistent; do not merge different rating scales or infer private mental
states from calendar titles, language style or sparse biometric signals. Describe what
the participant reported and changes with uncertainty. Workload is context, not diagnosis.

Coach may discuss the participant's chosen manageable routine or practical scheduling
preferences. Do not label a mental disorder or replace therapy. Explicit immediate danger
is handled by the host's prompt, empathetic human-care response, not by routine monitoring
or agent-task scheduling. Never promise crisis surveillance or invent local emergency contacts.

## Goals, habits and practical constraints

Inputs: goal in the person's words, chosen routine, preferences, constraints, previous
choices and reported barriers. Preserve “planned,” “accepted,” “done” and “unknown” separately.
An incomplete log is not noncompliance. Explore a barrier only when it can help the next
decision; avoid repeated check-ins that simply fill state fields.

Coach compares a few feasible options, explains fit, and asks for the meaningful choice.
Maintaining the current arrangement or ending a task is valid. Follow-up conditions and
service success are personalized. Analyst joins only when an actual evidence question
matters, not as an obligatory stage before every suggestion.

## Cross-domain reasoning

An association between sleep records and meeting-heavy days can be an observation with
several explanations. Source quality, changes in recording and missing data may explain
it. Preserve uncertainty and seek existing authorized context before questioning the person.
Do not export all domains to every role. Do not change a plan because a plausible hypothesis
was repeated in several reports. Evaluate any suggestion's delivery, execution and observed
changes independently. The best outcome may be a narrower, well-supported answer.
